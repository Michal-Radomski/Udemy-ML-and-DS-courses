In statistics and time series analysis, **covariance** is a measure that tells you how two different variables change
together. It looks at whether they tend to move in the same direction, opposite directions, or if there is no apparent
relationship between them at all.

### Understanding the Direction

When you look at the covariance between two variables (let's call them $X$ and $Y$):

- **Positive Covariance:** When $X$ goes up, $Y$ also tends to go up. For example, the amount of time you spend studying and
  your test scores usually have a positive covariance.
- **Negative Covariance:** When $X$ goes up, $Y$ tends to go down. For example, the number of hours you spend playing video
  games the night before an exam and your test scores might have a negative covariance.
- **Zero or Near-Zero Covariance:** The variables move independently of each other. Knowing what $X$ is tells you nothing
  about what $Y$ will do.

### Covariance vs. Correlation

While covariance tells you the _direction_ of the relationship, it has one major limitation: **its value depends on the scale
of the data**. If you measure something in meters instead of centimeters, the covariance value changes drastically, making it
hard to judge how strong the relationship actually is.

That is why statisticians often use **correlation**, which takes covariance and scales it between $-1$ and $+1$ so you can
easily see the strength of the relationship.

### Why Covariance Matters in Time Series

In time series forecasting, a special version called **autocovariance** comes into play:

- Instead of comparing two _different_ variables, autocovariance compares a variable with **itself at a different point in
  time** (e.g., today's value compared to yesterday's value).
- This helps forecasters figure out if past data has any useful memory or pattern that can help predict future values (which
  is a key part of checking for stationarity and building models).
