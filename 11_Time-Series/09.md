In time series analysis, **ACF (Autocorrelation Function)** and **PACF (Partial Autocorrelation Function)** are two essential
statistical tools. They are used to look at a time series and figure out how past values influence future values, which helps
forecasters choose the right model.

Building on your understanding of **covariance** and **stationarity**, ACF and PACF take autocorrelation a step further to
reveal the "memory" of your data.

---

### 1. ACF (Autocorrelation Function)

**ACF** measures the total correlation between a time series and a lagged version of itself across different time steps
(lags).

- **What it looks at:** If you want to see how much today's value ($y_t$) relates to yesterday's value ($y_{t-1}$), that's
  lag 1. How does it relate to the value from 5 days ago ($y_{t-5}$)? That's lag 5.
- **The Catch:** ACF measures **total** correlation. This means if today is strongly related to yesterday, and yesterday is
  strongly related to two days ago, ACF will show a strong relationship between today and two days ago—even if the direct
  connection has faded, because it includes the indirect "knock-on" effect.

### 2. PACF (Partial Autocorrelation Function)

**PACF** measures the correlation between a time series and a lagged version of itself, but **removes the effect of
everything in between**.

- **What it looks at:** It isolates the _direct_ relationship between two points in time. For example, when looking at the
  relationship between today and 3 days ago, PACF strips out the influence of days 1 and 2 to see if day 3 has any unique,
  direct impact left over.
- **The Analogy:** Imagine a game of telephone. ACF measures the total message that gets passed from person A to person D
  (including what B and C added or changed). PACF tries to figure out what person A whispered _directly_ to person D if you
  ignore the people in the middle.

---

### Why ACF and PACF Matter in Forecasting (ARIMA Models)

When building advanced forecasting models like **ARIMA** (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average), forecasters plot ACF and
PACF graphs (called **correlograms**) to figure out the model's structure:

- **AR (Autoregressive) terms:** The **PACF** graph helps determine how many past lags directly affect the current value. If
  PACF drops sharply to zero after lag 2, it suggests an AR(2) model.
- **MA (Moving Average) terms:** The **ACF** graph helps determine how past white noise shocks affect the series. If ACF
  drops sharply after lag 1, it suggests an MA(1) model.
