While standard time series models like ARMA and ARIMA forecast the _mean_ value (the expected trend or level) over time,
financial and economic data often experience periods of high volatility followed by periods of calm—a phenomenon known as
**volatility clustering**.

ARCH and GARCH models are designed specifically to forecast and model this changing **variance** (risk or volatility) over
time rather than the value itself.

---

### 1. ARCH (Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity)

- **What it stands for:** Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity
- **Core idea:** Financial returns often have periods where risk spikes dramatically followed by quiet periods. ARCH assumes
  that the current variance (risk) depends on **past squared error terms (shocks)** from previous periods.
- _Heteroskedasticity_ means that the variance is not constant over time; _conditional_ means the variance depends on past
  information.

- **Key parameter:** $p$ (the number of past squared errors used to predict current volatility).
- **Limitation:** Standard ARCH models often require a large number of lags ($p$) to capture volatility persistence
  accurately, which can make the model overly complex.

### 2. GARCH (Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity)

- **What it stands for:** Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity
- **Core idea:** Developed as an extension of ARCH, GARCH is much like an ARMA model applied to variance. Instead of relying
  only on past shocks, GARCH assumes that current volatility depends on **both past squared errors (shocks)** _and_ **past
  conditional variances (previous volatility levels)**. This allows the model to capture long-term persistence in volatility
  with fewer parameters.
- **Key parameters:** $(p, q)$, where:
- $p$ is the lag of the conditional variance (past volatility).
- $q$ is the lag of the squared residuals (past shocks).

- **Common Use Case:** $\text{GARCH}(1,1)$ is the industry standard in finance for modeling stock market volatility, option
  pricing, and risk management (such as calculating Value at Risk).
