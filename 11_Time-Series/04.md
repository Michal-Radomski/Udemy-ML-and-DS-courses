In time series forecasting, **white noise** refers to a sequence of random data points that have no pattern, trend, or
relationship with one another. Think of it like the static sound of an untuned radio—completely random and unpredictable.

### Core Properties of White Noise

For a time series to be considered white noise, it must satisfy three main conditions:

1. **Zero Mean:** The average value of the series across time is zero (or a constant, but usually centered at zero).
2. **Constant Variance:** The spread or variability of the data stays consistent over time (it doesn't suddenly get wilder or
   flatter).
3. **No Autocorrelation:** The values are completely independent. Knowing what happened yesterday tells you _nothing_ about
   what will happen today or tomorrow.

### Why Does It Matter in Forecasting?

White noise is the ultimate roadblock for forecasters, but it's also a valuable checkpoint:

- **You Can't Forecast It:** Because future white noise values are completely random and independent of past values, the best
  forecast for the next data point is simply zero (or the historical mean). You cannot predict what comes next.
- **The "Gold Standard" for Residuals:** When data scientists build forecasting models, they look at the leftover errors
  (called **residuals**). If a model successfully captures all the trends and patterns, the residuals should look like
  **white noise**. If the residuals still have patterns, it means the model missed something and needs tweaking.
