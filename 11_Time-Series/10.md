Building on everything we've covered so far—from white noise and stationarity to ACF and PACF—we arrive at one of the core
pillars of time series forecasting: the **Autoregressive (AR) Model**.

### What is an Autoregressive Model?

The term **Autoregressive** breaks down into two parts:

- **Auto:** Self (meaning the variable regresses against _itself_, not another variable).
- **Regressive:** Using past values to predict future values (like linear regression, but using past time steps as the
  independent variables).

In plain English, an AR model assumes that **future values depend linearly on past values**. If you want to predict today's
temperature, an AR model looks at yesterday's temperature, the day before's temperature, and so on, to make a forecast.

---

### The Anatomy of an AR(p) Model

An Autoregressive model is denoted as **AR($p$)**, where $p$ stands for the **order** of the model (how many past lags it
looks back at).

The general equation looks like this:

$$y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \epsilon_t$$

Where:

- $y_t$ is the value at the current time step (today).
- $c$ is a constant.
- $\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots, \phi_p$ are the coefficients (the "weights" assigned to each past day).
- $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}$ are the past values (lags).
- $\epsilon_t$ is a **white noise** shock (the unpredictable random error at time $t$).

#### Examples:

- **AR(1) Model:** Today's value depends _only_ on yesterday's value plus a white noise shock
  ($y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$).
- **AR(2) Model:** Today's value depends on both yesterday and two days ago
  ($y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \epsilon_t$).

---

### How PACF Helps Build an AR Model

Remember our discussion on **PACF (Partial Autocorrelation Function)**? This is where it pays off!

When you want to figure out what order $p$ to use for an AR model, you look at the PACF plot:

- The PACF shows the _direct_ relationship between a time series and its lagged versions.
- If a PACF plot cuts off sharply after **lag 2**, it tells you that only the past two days have a direct influence on today,
  pointing straight to an **AR(2)** model.

### Key Requirements for an AR Model

1. **Stationarity:** AR models require the time series to be **stationary**. If your data has an upward trend or a changing
   variance (like a random walk), an AR model will give unreliable predictions because the relationship ($\phi$ weights) will
   shift over time.
2. **Memory:** It assumes the past has a direct, decaying influence on the future.
