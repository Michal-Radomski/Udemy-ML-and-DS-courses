Building on everything you’ve learned so far—especially **stationarity** and the **Autoregressive (AR) model**—we can look at
how **ARMA** and **ARIMA** models expand on these ideas to build powerful forecasts.

The short answer: **The main difference is the "I" in ARIMA.** ARMA assumes your data is already stationary, while ARIMA can
handle non-stationary data by adding a differencing step.

---

### 1. ARMA($p, q$) — Autoregressive Moving Average

An ARMA model combines two parts:

- **AR ($p$):** The Autoregressive part (using past values to predict the future, like we just covered).
- **MA ($q$):** The Moving Average part (using past white noise error shocks to smooth out predictions).

#### The Big Catch with ARMA:

ARMA models **require the time series to be stationary**. If your data has an upward trend, a downward trend, or a changing
mean over time (like a random walk), an ARMA model will fail or give poor predictions because it expects the series to
fluctuate around a stable average.

---

### 2. ARIMA($p, d, q$) — Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

ARIMA takes the ARMA model and upgrades it by adding the **"Integrated" ($d$)** component.

- **The "Integrated" ($d$) Step:** This refers to **differencing** (subtracting the previous period's value from the current
  value). If your raw data is non-stationary, you apply differencing $d$ times until the data becomes stationary.
- Once the data is successfully made stationary through differencing, the model applies the standard AR($p$) and MA($q$)
  parts to make the forecast.

---

### Quick Comparison

| Feature              | ARMA($p, q$)                         | ARIMA($p, d, q$)                                                  |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Components**       | AR ($p$) + MA ($q$)                  | AR ($p$) + **Differencing ($d$)** + MA ($q$)                      |
| **Data Requirement** | Data **must** be stationary          | Can handle **non-stationary** data                                |
| **Real-World Use**   | Best for stable, mean-reverting data | Best for real-world data with trends (e.g., stock prices, GDP)    |
| **Parameters**       | $p$ (lag order), $q$ (error order)   | $p$ (lag order), **$d$ (differencing degree)**, $q$ (error order) |
