In time series analysis and forecasting, **stationarity** is one of the most important concepts. A time series is considered
**stationary** if its statistical properties—such as its mean (average), variance (spread), and autocorrelation—do not change
over time.

In plain English: a stationary series behaves consistently. It fluctuates around a constant long-term average, its volatility
stays roughly the same, and it doesn't wander off on a permanent trend.

### The Analogy: Stable vs. Wandering

- **Stationary Process (A Stable Thermostat):** Imagine the temperature inside a well-regulated house over a year. It might
  fluctuate up and down day-to-day, but it always centers around a stable average (e.g., 21°C) with consistent variability.
- **Non-Stationary Process (A Random Walk / City Growth):** Think of a city's population over decades, or a random walk. It
  grows or drifts over time, meaning its mean changes completely depending on _when_ you look at it.

### Why Stationarity Matters So Much

Most traditional forecasting models (like ARIMA) **depend on stationarity** to work effectively.

- **Predictable Patterns:** If a process is stationary, the statistical patterns it showed in the past (like how today
  relates to yesterday) will hold true in the future.
- **Model Failure on Non-Stationary Data:** If you feed non-stationary data (like a random walk with an upward trend)
  straight into a basic model, the model will often produce nonsense forecasts or assume trends will continue forever when
  they might crash tomorrow.

### How to Make Non-Stationary Data Stationary

If your data isn't stationary (which is true for most real-world data like stock prices or economic indicators), forecasters
usually transform it before modeling. The most common trick is **differencing**—looking at the _change_ from one period to
the next rather than the raw value itself. (Remember, taking the difference of a random walk gives you white noise!)
